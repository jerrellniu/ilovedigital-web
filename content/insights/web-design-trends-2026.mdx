**TL;DR**

- A good small-business website in 2026 tells a stranger what you do, where you do it and what to do next, all on the first screen.
- It loads quickly on a phone, it shows real proof, and every page has one clear button, because those three things are what turn a visitor into an enquiry.
- It is written so that Google and AI tools can read it, which mostly means plain headings and a separate page for each service.
- You can skip 3D, augmented reality and most of what the trend lists recommend, because none of it books a job.

## What makes a website good for a small business?

A good website for a trades or service business is a fast, plain, honest page that a customer can use one-handed on a phone. It says what you do and where you do it, it shows proof that you are real and good at your work, and it gives the visitor one obvious thing to do. The 2025 version of this article was a list of ten design trends, including immersive 3D, augmented reality and neumorphism, and in years of building websites for Queensland businesses I have never seen any of them win a customer a job. So this year the list is shorter, every item on it has to earn its place, and it is the same standard we build to on our own [websites](/websites).

## 1. Does the first screen say what you do and where?

It should, because a visitor decides within seconds whether they are in the right place. When we audit a site we hand it to someone who does not know the business and give them five seconds on a phone, and if they cannot say what the business does and where, the page has failed. The fix is almost always the words rather than the design. A home page that says "Plumber on the Gold Coast, book online or call" has done the job in one line, and if yours does not, start by [rewriting the copy](/insights/writing-great-website-copy).

## 2. Does it load quickly on a phone?

Test it, because most of your customers will find you on a phone, often on a job site or in a car park with a weak signal, and speed decides whether they wait or go back to the search results. Google publishes the targets it measures against: the main image should appear within 2.5 seconds, a tap should get a response within 200 milliseconds, and the page should not jump around as it loads. PageSpeed Insights tests your site against those numbers for free. The usual cause of a slow site is photos uploaded straight from a phone at four or five megabytes each, and the second is a theme carrying twenty features you never use. Both can be fixed without rebuilding the site, and a site that passes keeps the visitors a slow site loses.

## 3. Does every page have one job and one button?

It should, because a visitor given several choices usually makes none. Every page needs a single next step, and the button for it should be visible without scrolling and say what happens when you press it, whether that is "Book a class", "Get a quote" or "Call now". If the job is bookings, put the booking calendar on the page itself, because sending someone to a separate portal loses a share of them at every hop. If the job is quotes, ask for the three things you need in order to quote and nothing more, and more people will finish the form.

## 4. Does it show proof?

It has to, because people believe other people and nobody believes a website that says it offers quality workmanship. Show the workmanship instead.

- **Use real photos.** Take real photos of the van, the team and the finished job. Your own photos are far more convincing than stock photos of models in hard hats, because they show a real business the visitor can picture turning up.
- **Quote reviews word for word.** Copy the Google review exactly, with the person's name and their star rating, and do not tidy it up. We check every quote on our own site against the original before it goes live, because an edited review is a fake review and readers can tell.
- **Publish case studies.** Say what the problem was, what you did and what changed, using numbers where you have them and inventing nothing where you do not. Ours are on the [work page](/work) if you want a format to borrow.
- **Cover the basics of trust.** Put a real address, a phone number that rings and an ABN in the footer, and make sure the padlock shows in the browser bar. Each one answers the question a cautious visitor is silently asking.

## 5. Can Google and AI read it?

They can if it is written plainly, and this is the part that has changed most since the last version of this article. People now ask Google's AI Mode, ChatGPT and Gemini the questions they used to type into a search box, and Google's own guidance is that there is nothing special to do for AI features because the same things that help you rank also help you get quoted. In practice that means the following.

- **Write headings as questions**, with the answer in the first sentence underneath, because that is the shape an AI answer is built from.
- **Give each service and each area you serve its own page.** "Blocked drains in Southport" deserves its own page rather than one line on the services page, because Google ranks pages rather than businesses.
- **Add a short FAQ to each service page**, with three to five real questions, so there is question-shaped content to match.
- **Keep your business details matching** across your site, your Google Business Profile and every directory, because both Google and AI tools cross-check them.
- **Add structured data** that tells Google what kind of business you are, your hours and your service area. Most platforms add it for you, and it is worth having someone check.

## 6. Can you update it yourself?

You should be able to, because a site you cannot change is costing you more than it should. If changing your opening hours means emailing a developer and waiting three days, the platform is the problem. A good site in 2026 sits on something you can log into and change yourself, whether that is Squarespace, Shopify, WordPress with a decent host, or a custom build with a content system behind it. Every build we do includes training, so the owner can change a price, add a photo and post an update without asking anyone. The one warning is to know [when a redesign is due](/insights/when-to-redesign-your-website) before the platform becomes the reason you cannot change anything.

## 7. Do you own it?

Check, because every year I see a business try to leave a provider and discover it does not own its own website. The domain should be registered in your name, the hosting account should be yours, and the Google Business Profile should be owned by you rather than by your last web developer. Before you sign anything, ask who will own the domain, the site and the accounts when the engagement ends, and do not sign until the answer is you.

## What can you skip?

You can skip 3D and augmented reality, dark mode toggles and animated effects, and chatbots that cannot answer anything. The first two are slow and expensive, and nobody chooses a plumber because the website had a 3D model. A chatbot is only worth having if it can book a job or give a price, and if it cannot, it is a slower version of the contact form. Spend that money on photos of your real work instead, because the photos will win you jobs and the effects will not.

## The five-minute test

Run this tonight, on your phone, with your own site.

1. Can a stranger tell what you do and where within five seconds?
2. Does the page load before you get impatient?
3. Is there one obvious button, and does it work?
4. Is there a real photo and a real review on the first screen of the home page?
5. Can you log in right now and change your opening hours?

If you answered yes to all five, your site is doing its job. If you answered yes to fewer than three, it is time to talk.

## FAQs

**How much does a small business website cost?**
It depends on the platform and on how many pages you need, and we do not publish price lists because a two-page site and a twenty-page site are different jobs. [Ask for a quote](/contact) and you will get a fixed figure rather than a range.

**Should I use Squarespace, Shopify or WordPress?**
Squarespace suits a service business that wants to manage its own site, Shopify suits anyone selling products, and WordPress suits a business that needs something neither of those can do and has someone to maintain it. Beyond that point, a custom build starts to make sense.

**How long does a website take to build?**
A service business site usually takes four to six weeks from kickoff to launch, and most of that time is spent waiting on photos and copy from the owner. If you have those ready, it goes faster.

**Do I need a blog?**
You need pages that answer the questions customers ask you, and whether you call that a blog does not matter. One useful article a month does more than twenty pages nobody reads.

**Is my current site good enough?**
Run the five-minute test above, and if you want a second opinion, a [free website audit](/audit) will tell you exactly what is missing and what is worth fixing first.
