## Is it against the rules to ask customers for Google reviews?

Asking a genuine customer for an honest Google review is a normal part of running a business. Nothing in the rules covered here stops you doing it.

The legal line in Australia sits somewhere else. According to the [ACCC's guidance on online reviews](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/online-reviews-for-product-and-services), it is against Australian Consumer Law for a business to create fake or misleading reviews, or to arrange for others to do so.

Google also sets its own rules for reviews on Maps, and those sit separately from the law. Think of it as two tests: the law asks whether you misled anyone, and Google asks whether you followed its house rules.

Pass both tests and you can ask every customer, on every job, with a clear conscience.

## When is the best time to ask for a review?

I recommend asking in person at handover, while the customer is standing at the finished job and the work is fresh in their mind.

Send the link by text the same day, ideally before you've left the street. A request that arrives a week later has to compete with everything else in their week.

If they haven't left a review after a few days, send one friendly reminder and then stop.

For a clinic, the moment is at the front desk after the appointment. For a café, it is when the bill is settled or the customer says something nice about the food.

Asking while the job is fresh turns a favour into an easy yes.

## Which channel works best for a tradie, a clinic or a café?

In person followed by SMS suits most trades, because you meet every customer face to face and already have their mobile number. Other businesses need a slightly different mix, so match the channel to how your customers actually deal with you.

Keep the ask one-to-one. A general social media post asking for reviews reaches people who may never have used you, which is the opposite of what a review is for.

Pick the row that matches your business and you'll know exactly when and how to ask.

## How do I get my direct Google review link or QR code?

Your direct review link comes from your Google Business Profile. Sign in to your profile, look for the option to ask for or share reviews, and copy the link it gives you.

Google changes its interface from time to time, so if the menu looks different, search the Google Business Profile Help centre for its current steps on sharing a review link.

Save that link as a text shortcut on your phone and in your job management software, so it goes out with one tap.

To make a QR code, paste the same link into a QR code generator, then print the code on your invoice, quote template, business card or a sticker in the van.

If your listing isn't fully set up yet, start with [our Google Business Profile guide for Gold Coast tradies](/insights/google-business-profile-optimisation), because every review you earn lands on that listing.

With one link ready to go, asking for a review takes almost no effort at all.

## What should I actually say when I ask?

Say it plainly, say it once, and make it about helping other locals find a good business. These are the scripts I give my clients, and they're ready to copy.

- In person at handover, say: 'Glad you're happy with it. An honest review really helps other locals find us. Can I text you the link now?'
- If most of your work genuinely comes from Google, you can say so instead, but only use a line like that if it is true for your business, because a review request should never tell the customer something misleading.
- By SMS straight after the job, send: 'Hi [Name], thanks for having us today. If you've got a minute, an honest Google review helps other locals find us: [link]. Cheers, [Your name], [Business]. Reply STOP to opt out.'
- By email for professional services, write: 'Hi [Name], it was a pleasure helping with [job]. If you have a moment, an honest review on Google helps other people decide whether we're right for them. Here is the direct link: [link]. Thank you, [Your name].'
- For a reminder a few days later, send: 'Hi [Name], just a quick nudge on that Google review link from [Business], no stress if you're busy: [link].'
- On a QR card at the counter, print: 'Happy with your visit? Tell other locals on Google.'

## Why does the word 'honest' appear in every script?

The word 'honest' tells the customer you want their real view, good or bad. It also makes the request much easier to say out loud, because you're asking for an opinion rather than a favour.

The same plain-words approach works across your whole site, and [our guide to writing great website copy](/insights/writing-great-website-copy) shows you how to apply it.

An honest request is easier to make and easier to say yes to.

## Should my staff ask, and should they use the same script?

Your staff should ask, because the person who finishes the job is the person the customer has just watched do the work.

Give the whole team one script and one link, and have everyone send from the same shortcut. A consistent script means every customer hears the same honest request, and nobody improvises an offer you'd rather they hadn't made.

Agree as a team that nobody writes, edits or posts a review on a customer's behalf, and nobody offers anything in return for one.

A shared script turns review requests into a habit across every van, rather than a job left to the owner.

## How do I set it all up this week?

Set up the link and the shortcut on day one, brief the team in the first week, and start a monthly check in the first month. The checklist below is the order I take clients through, and it has no review-count targets on purpose, because the goal is a habit rather than a number.

- On day one, copy your direct review link from your Google Business Profile and save it as a text shortcut on your phone.
- On day one, add the SMS script and the link to the message templates in your job management software, so the request goes out with the job completion.
- In the first week, run a short staff briefing where everyone gets the same script, the same link and the same rules on incentives and writing reviews.
- In the first week, add the link to your invoice template and print the QR code for your quote template, business cards and the van.
- In the first month, check your listing once against the businesses beside you in the map pack for your main service and suburb, and note how your recent reviews compare.
- From then on, repeat that check monthly and keep asking every customer at every handover.

## Is it legal to text customers asking for reviews in Australia?

Commercial texts in Australia are covered by the Spam Act 2003, which the Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) enforces. The Act is built around three things: consent, clear identification of who is sending the message, and a working way to unsubscribe.

A review request is a business message, so I treat it with the same care as any marketing text. Whether a number collected for a job counts as consent is a question for ACMA's own spam guidance for businesses, so read it before you automate review texts at scale.

The list below is the practice I recommend to my clients, built around those three points.

- Only text people who gave you their number as part of the job, and tell them at handover that the text is coming, so the customer has agreed to it in person.
- Name your business in every message, so the customer knows exactly who it's from.
- Include a simple way to opt out, such as 'Reply STOP', and honour it straight away.
- Send one request and no more than one reminder.
- Send during normal working hours, because a text late at night reads as careless whatever the rules say.

## What about the 'quiet hours' rule other guides mention?

Some SMS guides state a fixed daily texting window as settled Australian law. Treat any rule like that with care unless the guide points you to the Spam Act 2003 or ACMA itself.

Before you automate review texts at scale, read ACMA's own spam guidance for businesses, or get proper advice.

Our list above is our practice, not legal advice. Following it keeps your texts welcome and keeps your business name off anyone's complaint list.

## Can I offer a discount or giveaway for a review?

Australian Consumer Law allows it only in limited circumstances, and my advice is not to.

The ACCC warns that businesses offering incentives for positive reviews risk misleading consumers and breaching the law. Any incentive must apply regardless of whether the review is good or bad, and it must be clearly disclosed.

Google sets its own rules for reviews on Maps, and meeting the ACCC test does not make an incentive acceptable on Google. Read Google's current Maps content policy in the Google Business Profile Help centre before you offer anything at all.

A prize draw for five-star reviewers only fails the ACCC test outright. Even an incentive offered to every reviewer and clearly disclosed is not something I would put a client's listing behind.

Skip the incentive and ask well instead. Reviews earned with a good job and a polite request are the ones you never have to worry about.

## Can I ask only my happy customers?

Ask every customer. Choosing who gets the link based on whether you expect a good review gives future customers a filtered picture, and my position for every client is that everyone gets the link.

On the Australian Consumer Law side, the ACCC's HealthEngine case shows how seriously suppression is treated. HealthEngine was ordered to pay a $2.9 million penalty, including for suppressing and editing negative patient reviews.

What you should do is fix problems before you ask. If a job went wrong, sort it out first, then ask once it's right, the same as you would anyone else.

Asking everyone protects your business and gives future customers a picture they can trust.

## What has the ACCC actually penalised?

The ACCC has taken action over fake and manipulated reviews with penalties ranging from a few thousand dollars to millions. The ACCC sets out the three outcomes below in its online reviews guidance, linked in the first section of this post.

*ACCC enforcement outcomes on online reviews (source: ACCC online reviews guidance)*

| Business | What they did | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citymove | Published fake testimonials copied from an unrelated site | Infringement notice and a $6,600 penalty paid |
| Service Seeking | Let businesses draft and self-publish their own 'customer' reviews | Federal Court ordered a $600,000 penalty |
| HealthEngine | Penalty included suppressing and editing negative patient reviews | Ordered to pay a $2.9 million penalty |

## What does that mean for a small business?

Citymove's case is the one to remember. Copying testimonials from somewhere else can feel like a harmless shortcut, and it still ended in an infringement notice and a $6,600 penalty.

Every review should come from a real customer, in their own words, about a job you actually did.

We hold our own business to that standard. Every client testimonial we publish is a Google review the client left themselves, quoted word for word, because putting words in a client's mouth is a credibility risk.

We also don't mark those reviews up as review schema on our own site, because Google's structured data policy excludes self-serving reviews, meaning reviews about your own business shown on your own site, from rich results.

Keep every review genuine and you never have to wonder whether one could come back to bite you.

## What if a customer says yes but never leaves a review?

Send one friendly reminder a few days later, then let it go.

People forget, and one nudge with the link attached is helpful. A second or third starts to feel like pressure, and pressure is how a happy customer becomes an irritated one.

Put the link on the invoice and the QR code on the receipt, so the reminder is already in their hands without another message.

A light touch keeps the relationship intact, and that customer may still call you for the next job.

## What do I do about a fake or unfair review?

Reply calmly in public, report the review through your Google Business Profile, and never respond by posting fake reviews of your own.

A short, polite reply shows future customers how you handle problems. Thank them, state the facts briefly, and offer to sort it out offline.

If the review breaks Google's rules, for example it's from someone who was never a customer, report it from your profile. The Google Business Profile Help centre carries the current steps, and removal is Google's decision, so keep your reply professional either way.

Australian Consumer Law applies to every business, whatever its size. The ACCC guidance linked at the top of this post makes it unlawful for any business to create fake or misleading reviews or arrange for others to do so, so being a small operator is no exemption.

Writing reviews to drown out a bad one is exactly the kind of fake review conduct the ACCC has penalised.

A calm, factual reply is the part of the story you control, and it is the part future customers read.
