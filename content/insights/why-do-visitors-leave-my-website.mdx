Visitors leave your website within seconds when they can't tell what you do, the page is slow to load, or there's no obvious next step. All three are fixable, and none of them needs a big budget to check.

Before you spend anything on [website design for your trade business](/websites), it's worth knowing which of the 'seconds' figures online come from real research and which are opinion. This guide separates them, translates them for owners who are busy on the tools, and shows you what to fix first.

## Key Points

- Nielsen Norman Group research found the first 10 seconds of a page visit are when people are most likely to leave, and the average page visit lasts about 30 seconds.
- The three-second and five-second figures you see online come from agency articles rather than the same research, and they describe reasons people leave rather than a measured time limit.
- Google/SOASTA research shows the probability of a bounce increases by 32% as load time goes from 1 second to 3 seconds, and by 123% from 1 second to 10 seconds.
- Pfizer cut its page load time from 21 seconds to 5.2 seconds and reduced its bounce rate by 20%.
- A quick exit is not always a failure, because a customer who finds your number and taps to call has done exactly what you wanted.
- Google's guidance says a page must be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet before it can appear in AI Overviews, so a clear answer high on the page helps both visitors and AI tools.

## What counts as someone leaving your website?

Leaving can mean a few different things, and the numbers in your analytics only make sense once you know which one you're looking at.

- A bounce is when someone lands on one page and leaves without doing anything else, such as clicking to another page or tapping a button.
- An exit is simply the last page someone looked at before they left, so every visit ends with one, including visits that turned into a booking.
- A quick back is when someone taps your result on Google, glances at the page and hits the back button to try the next business on the list.
- The quick back is the one that costs you work, because it's a visitor who wanted what you sell and couldn't see it fast enough.
- Knowing the difference stops you panicking over a number that might actually be good news.

## How many seconds do you actually have?

You have roughly 10 seconds to convince someone to stay, according to the most solid research on the topic.

[Nielsen Norman Group's study of how long users stay on web pages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages) found the first 10 seconds are critical, because the chance of leaving is very high in those first moments. People are sceptical after years of landing on poorly designed pages, so they arrive half expecting to be disappointed.

The same research found users often leave within 10 to 20 seconds, but pages with a clear value proposition hold attention for much longer. The average page visit lasts about 30 seconds.

The three-second and five-second figures come from somewhere else. [A design agency's article on the first 3 seconds](https://www.3xd.design/blog/why-visitors-leave-your-site) argues the top reason people leave that early is they can't tell what the business does or what to do next.

[A web agency's list of reasons visitors leave in 5 seconds](https://www.foduu.com/blog/reasons-for-leaving-a-website) names slow loading, unclear messaging at the top of the page, poor visual hierarchy, a poor mobile experience, salesy or robotic writing, confusing navigation and no clear call to action.

Those articles make useful checklists, but they're opinion rather than measured research. They describe why people leave, not an exact stopwatch.

What matters for you is simple: your page needs to answer what you do, whether you're right for the job and how to book before that 10-second window closes.

## Why do people leave in those first few seconds?

People leave because the page makes them work too hard to find out whether you can help.

- Your headline doesn't say what you do. A headline like 'Quality you can trust' tells a homeowner nothing, while 'Hot water systems repaired and replaced across the Gold Coast' answers the question straight away and keeps them reading.
- Your page loads slowly. Someone standing in a flooded laundry won't wait around, and the speed research further down shows how quickly patience runs out.
- There's no obvious next step. If your phone number is buried in the footer, a customer who is ready to book has to hunt for it, and many won't.
- The layout is cluttered. When everything on the page competes for attention, the eye has nowhere to settle, so the thumb heads for the back button instead.
- The site is hard to use on a phone. Tiny text, buttons squashed together and pop-ups covering the screen all push people away.
- The writing sounds like a brochure. Copy full of 'industry-leading solutions' reads as salesy, while plain words about the actual job read like someone who does it every day.

## How much does a slow website cost you?

A slow website loses visitors before they've even seen your headline. Research by Google and SOASTA, reproduced in Google's [Milliseconds Make Millions report](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9757/Milliseconds_Make_Millions_report_hQYAbZJ.pdf), found the probability of a bounce rises steadily as load time increases.

The same report tracked brands that sped up their sites. BMW's mobile sales traffic share rose from 8% to 30%, Pfizer cut load time from 21 seconds to 5.2 seconds and reduced bounce rate by 20%, and TUI saw a 31% decrease in bounce rate alongside a 78% reduction in load times.

Those are big brands, but the principle applies just as well to a plumber in Southport. Speed is also part of the ordinary SEO work we do to help a page rank, so a faster site helps you get found as well as keep the people who arrive.

Every second you take off the load time gives more customers the chance to read about your work instead of your competitor's.

| --- | --- |

| 1 second to 5 seconds | 90% |
| 1 second to 6 seconds | 106% |
| 1 second to 10 seconds | 123% |

## Is a high bounce rate always bad?

No, a quick exit is sometimes the best result you can get. A customer who searches 'electrician Robina', lands on your page, sees your number and taps to call has left your website in seconds, and that's exactly what you wanted.

Depending on how your analytics are set up, that call can still show up as a bounce. The same goes for someone who checks your opening hours or service area, gets the answer and calls you the next morning.

I don't give clients a 'good' bounce rate to aim for, because I haven't seen a reliable benchmark for Australian trade sites. Compare your own pages month to month instead, and look at whether calls and enquiries are rising.

That way you're judging your website on the work it brings in, not on a percentage.

## Does this work differently on mobile?

Yes, and for a lot of trade businesses the phone is the version that matters most, because customers often search the moment something goes wrong at home.

On a phone, the top of the screen is all someone sees before they decide to stay. Your headline, your service area and a tap-to-call button need to fit there without scrolling.

Phones on patchy connections also feel a slow site more sharply. BMW's jump in mobile sales traffic share after its speed rebuild shows how strongly mobile visitors respond to a fast site.

Test your own site on your phone using mobile data, not the office wi-fi. If it works for a customer standing in their driveway, it will work everywhere.

## What happens when people arrive from an ad or your Google listing?

Visitors from ads and your Google listing are quick to leave when the page doesn't match what they clicked.

If your ad says 'Same-day air conditioning repairs, Gold Coast' and the link drops them on a generic homepage about new installs, they have to go looking for the repair service. Many won't bother.

Send each ad to the page that matches it, with the same words in the headline. Do the same for the website link on your Google listing, which our guide to [Google Business Profile optimisation for Gold Coast tradies](/insights/google-business-profile-optimisation) covers step by step.

When the page repeats the promise they clicked on, visitors know straight away they're in the right place.

## How do quick exits affect being found on Google and AI tools?

The same clarity that keeps visitors on your page also helps Google and AI tools understand and quote it. Google's own guidance says a page must be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet before it can appear in AI Overviews, and there are no special extra requirements.

That means AI answers draw on the same pool of pages that already rank. Our approach is to put the answer in plain, visible text high on the page, because that's the text both people and AI tools can read and lift.

A page that opens with 'We repair and replace hot water systems across the Gold Coast' works for a rushed homeowner, for Google's snippet and for an AI answer at the same time.

Write the page for your customer first, and you're writing it for Google and AI tools at the same time.

## What can you check in 20 minutes with no budget?

You can find most of the problems yourself with a phone, a friend and a cup of tea.

- Load your homepage on your phone using mobile data and notice how long it takes before you can read the headline. If you're waiting long enough to notice, your customers are too.
- Ask someone who doesn't know your business to look at your homepage for a few seconds, then answer three questions: what do you do, is it for me, and what do I do next. Any question they can't answer is your first fix.
- Check that your phone number or booking button is visible at the top of the page on a phone without scrolling, so a ready customer can act straight away.
- Read your headline out loud. If it could belong to any business in any town, rewrite it to name your service and your area.
- Swap stock photos for real ones. Take real photos of the van, the team and the finished job, because real photos are far more convincing to a visitor than stock images.
- Click through from your own Google listing and any ads you're running, and check each one lands on a page that matches what was promised.
- Run your homepage through Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool to see whether speed is a problem worth fixing.

## How do you measure this without being a data analyst?

Watch the things that pay the bills before the numbers in a dashboard. Track calls, quote requests and bookings each month, and ask new customers how they found you.

If you have Google Analytics set up, look at one thing: how long people stay on your key service pages, and whether that changes after you make a fix. Compare each page against itself over time rather than chasing someone else's benchmark.

Google Search Console is free and shows which searches bring people to your site and how often they click. A page that appears often in search but rarely gets clicked usually needs a clearer title and description.

Measure what changes after each fix, and you'll know which changes are bringing in work.
